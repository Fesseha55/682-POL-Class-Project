2.  Introduction

Race in the United States represents a historical and social construct rather than a natural fact, deliberately created over centuries to justify power structures and institutionalize inequality (The MIT Press reader). Beginning in the latter half of the twentieth century, racial categorization became a cornerstone of U.S. civil rights enforcement, voting rights protection, and resource allocation policy. Originally conceived as an instrument of equity-enabling federal agencies, courts, and policymakers to identify and remedy systemic racial disparities. The racial classification system has evolved into a politically contested terrain where partisan actors exploit racial categories to advance electoral objectives (Native American Rights Fund, 2024). As partisan polarization intensifies and demographic data increasingly inform electoral strategy and redistricting, the political consequences of racial categorization have grown exponentially more complex, with African Americans experiencing disproportionate exposure to polarizing political practices (Redistricting Information, 2021).
This research confronts a fundamental paradox in American democracy: while racial categories ostensibly serve equity purposes, U.S. political parties actively perpetuate racial polarization by weaponizing these categories to mobilize voters through racialized appeals, suppress minority electoral participation, and advance divergent policy agendas affecting African American communities (How Voter Suppression Legislation Is Tied to Race, 2026). The Republican Party’s Southern Strategy systematically deployed coded racial language appealing to white resentment, while Democrats increasingly emphasized civil rights protection, creating a bifurcated party system organized around racial identity (Encyclopedia Britannica. (n.d.). Southern strategy.). Contemporary manifestations include explicit voter suppression targeting Black voters, rhetorical appeals exploiting racial resentments, and strategic policy divergence on race-salient issues. These mechanisms suggest that political party competition has become fundamentally structured around racial polarization rather than representing a neutral expression of voter preferences.

5.1. The Historical Development of Racial Polarization in American Politics

The term “race” was first used in English in the late 16th century, with a general meaning similar to type, sort or kind. By the 18th century, the term began to categorize and rank people in the English colonies, distinguishing between Europeans, Indigenous Americans, and Africans brought as enslaved labor. This categorization has continued into modern times. Race now refers to biological variation, including phenotypical differences in stature, skin color, hair color, facial shape, and other inherent characteristics that may or may not be mutually exclusive to everyone (Rose P. 2018 pp. 47-48).
In the United States, the race “black” categorizes anyone with any African ancestry (Mixed Race America - Who Is Black? One Nation’s Definition | Jefferson’s Blood | FRONTLINE | PBS, 2015; Tian et al., 2025). This definition of black identity is specific to the United States and is not found in other countries, where definitions of who is black can differ greatly. One-drop rule (a one drop of black blood) is a historical American social and legal principle holding that any person with even one ancestor of Black African ancestry is considered Black. This includes individuals with small percentages of African ancestry, like one-fourth or one-eighth. The rule originated in the American South, influenced by the history of slavery and segregation, and helped enforce a rigid social hierarchy. Terms like mulatto, colored, Negro, Afro-American and African American have been used to describe people of African heritage. Mulatto once meant the child of one black and one white parent but is encompassed under the broader term of black. This racial polarization is widely accepted in American society, recognized and institutionalized in federal and state courts, practiced daily in the U.S. political parties. 
U.S. political parties have historically mobilized around racial divisions, with significant shifts before and after the Civil Rights Movement shaping modern polarization (Bateman, 2019). Before the American civil war in the era 1785-1868, debates over Black suffrage revealed early partisan sorting. Jeffersonians and Democrats increasingly opposed the enfranchisement of free Black men, framing it as a threat to slavery and white supremacy, while Federalists and later Whigs offered inconsistent support. This divide deepened after the Civil War, as Republicans positioning themselves as the “Party of Lincoln” championed Black political rights and secured overwhelming Black electoral support during Reconstruction.
However, the mid-20th century marked a critical realignment. Black voters largely remained loyal to the Republican Party until Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal began to shift allegiances toward the Democrats (For Black Republicans, a Dramatic Shift | UW College of Arts & Sciences, n.d.). By the 1950s, Republican candidates like Dwight Eisenhower still captured 25-32% of the Black vote, but this support eroded rapidly following Barry Goldwater’s opposition to the Civil Rights Act of 1964. This moment accelerated partisan racial sorting, reinforced by Richard Nixon’s “Southern Strategy,” which relied on coded appeals to white voters resistant to desegregation and helped transform the South into a Republican stronghold.
Ronald Reagan further institutionalized this approach in the 1980s, combining racially coded rhetoric with selective promotion of Black conservatives to legitimize opposition to policies such as affirmative action, while marginalizing more moderate voices within the party. These dynamics continue in contemporary politics, where racialized narratives such as claims of election fraud disproportionately targeting Black and Latino communities in 2020 reflect and reinforce enduring patterns of partisan polarization.

5.2	 Institutional Mechanisms perpetuating Racial Polarization

Parties perpetuate polarization through mechanisms like voter suppression, gerrymandering, messaging, and policy divergence. Post-Reconstruction Democrats used Jim Crow literacy tests and poll taxes to disenfranchise Blacks; modern Republicans employ voter ID laws and purges disproportionately affecting minorities. Gerrymandering packs Black voters into few districts (packing) or dilutes them across many (cracking), minimizing representation-as seen in post-1965 Southern redistricting.
Campaign messaging deploys dog-whistles: Nixon’s ‘law and order’ coded anti-Black crime fears, while Trump’s narratives implicitly blamed non-white urban voters. Policy divergence amplifies this that Democrats champion race-conscious remedies like affirmative action, while Republicans frame them as ‘reverse discrimination,’ mobilizing white resentment. Antebellum slaveholder influence mirrored this, pressuring Northern Democrats against Black suffrage to preserve racial hierarchy.

5.3. Racial polarization-a strategic maneuvering for political parties

Racial polarization is viewed as a losing strategy for America but serves as a short-term winning tactic for both political parties, helping them gain votes and power at the cost of long-term trust in elections. 
In the 1960s and 1970s, Republicans adopted the ‘Southern Strategy,’ focusing on white Southerners discontent with civil rights changes and school integration, leading to a significant shift in electoral power from Democrats to Republicans. This strategic shift helped the GOP secure control of the presidency and Congress by appealing to a larger base of white voters. 
Both parties utilize racial appeals to energize their supporters; Republicans target whites with racial resentment to encourage turnout in rural areas, while Democrats rely on the solid loyalty of Black voters, who consistently support them. In 2020, false claims of voter fraud in predominantly Black cities suppressed urban minority voter turnout. 
Historically, this approach is not new, mirroring strategies from the antebellum era when Democrats prioritized white coalitions over Black voting rights. Additionally, Republicans use prominent Black conservatives to soften critical discussions on race. However, the erosion of trust in democracy is a significant hidden cost of these tactics, as parties prioritize immediate electoral gains over long-term democratic health.

5.4.	Consequence evaluation of Racially Polarized U.S. Politics

Party-driven polarization hurts Black political power, representation, and results in clear ways that everyday people can see. Simply having more Black lawmakers in Congress doesn’t win over white voters. In fact, the longer whites see Black leaders in charge, the more resentment builds up. This drops those leaders’ approval ratings and cuts Black candidates’ votes (For Black Republicans, a Dramatic Shift | UW College of Arts & Sciences, n.d.).
Back in the 1800s before the Civil War, laws blocked Black people from voting, locking in white control. Today, gerrymandering crams most Black voters into just a few ‘super-Black’ districts, wasting their votes statewide and blocking progress on things like voting rights or affirmative action (Edwards, n.d.). On the social side, this creates deep emotional divides: whites living in districts led by Black politicians often grow more angry over time. It also damages democracy itself like the claims of 2020 election that fraud aimed at Black neighborhoods widened party hatred and broke trust in elections for everyone (CNN, 2020). After the Civil War, Black power grew briefly during Reconstruction, but the South crushed it with poll taxes and ‘literacy’ tests. Modern rules like strict voter ID and barring ex-felons from voting do the same thing today, blocking Black wins on key policies (Bates, 2014b).

5.5.	Future trajectory analysis of Racially Polarized U.S. Politics

Different literatures show that America’s future voters younger and more diverse are starting to shake up racial divides in politics. Non-white youth strongly favor Democrats, boosting blue areas like cities, while older white seniors keep shifting Republican. This creates separated voting groups by age and race, which could lock polarization in place for generations if nothing changes.
Black support for Republicans might tick up slightly, with post-2024 Trump data showing hints of 14-19% leaning that way, especially among younger Black men. But bigger party switches face tough headwinds. Meanwhile, Supreme Court rulings against extreme racial gerrymandering and rising youth turnout could weaken tactics that suppress minority votes.
Real change could come from bipartisan reforms to reduce polarization or Black-led groups bridging party lines, like in the early days of the Republican Party. Without action, though, growing emotional hatred between parties will keep pushing people into racial voting blocs. Demographics offer hope, but segregation risks make the future uncertain.

6.	Methodology

This study employs three complementary theoretical frameworks to understand party-driven racial polarization. Social Identity Theory explains how party affiliation becomes fused with racial identity, creating affective polarization wherein voters viscerally dislike opposing parties associated with different racial groups. Multiculturalism Theory provides conceptual tools for understanding how democratic systems can recognize and accommodate racial diversity without enabling parties to weaponize racial categories for partisan advantage. Political Polarization Theory illuminates the mechanisms through which elite rhetoric, institutional design, and partisan competition intensify polarization across multiple dimensions like ideological, affective, and racial (Bates, 2014).
This study used a mixed methods research design that includes three main approaches: 
a.	Historical-Institutional Analysis: It reviews primary sources, party platforms, and legislation from 1960 to the present to show how political parties have used racial categories strategically. 
b.	Quantitative Analysis: It examines voter registration, turnout rates, suppression laws, and election results to measure the effects of polarization driven by political parties.
c.	Qualitative Analysis: It involves interviews with party operatives, voting rights advocates, and community members to understand their experiences with political polarization. 

7.	Theoretical Framework

According to Martin Lunt, the history of race is the story of a social and political instrument evolved over centuries to categorize human difference and naturalize social hierarchies (The MIT Press reader). While ancient societies, such as the Greeks and Romans, displayed varieties of xenophobia, these prejudices usually included an escape hatch through conversion or assimilation, while the concept of race as an immutable biological fact is a relatively modern invention. Political parties in the United States use race as a central tool for electoral mobilization, though they do so through fundamentally different frameworks regarding the causes of racial inequality (The Role of Racial Resentment in Our Politics, 2026b). 
In political science, polarization means the public and elites sort into increasingly distant, internally consistent camps with little overlap. It shows up in two main forms: Ideological polarization: Republicans and Democrats (and their voters) move farther apart on policy (taxes, immigration, climate, etc.), with fewer moderates in the middle. Affective polarization is also when people don’t just disagree; they dislike and distrust the other side more, seeing them as misguided or even threatening.
Polarized America by Alan Abramowitz, argues that voters and politicians have sorted along clear ideological and social lines, reinforcing each other over time. Work by Pew Research Center and scholars like Lilliana Mason adds that identities (party, religion, race, culture) now stack which are intensifying conflict. There is strong evidence that politics in the United States fits the pattern of polarization. In Congress, voting behavior has become sharply divided along party lines, with very little overlap between Democrats and Republicans compared to earlier periods; measures of legislative ideology show that the gap between the parties is at its widest in over a century. Among the public, surveys consistently reveal large and growing differences in opinion on major issues, with fewer people holding mixed or moderate views. Emotional or affective polarization is also evident, as many Americans express distrust or dislike toward the opposing party and are less willing to form personal relationships across political lines. Media environments contribute to this divide, as people increasingly consume information from ideologically aligned sources, reinforcing existing beliefs. At the same time, geographic patterns such as the divide between urban and rural areas have made electoral competition less balanced and reduced incentives for compromise. Taken together, these trends show that U.S. politics is widely considered highly polarized because differences are deeper, more consistent, and more emotionally charged than in the past. It’s not just disagreement over policy, it’s a widening divide in identity, trust, and perception of reality.

1. Introduction

This is a test case. The classification of race in United States demographic forms has long played a central role in shaping public policy, civil rights enforcement, and political representation. Among these classifications, the term “African American” emerged not only as a descriptive category but as a symbol of recognition, dignity, and inclusion. Intended to acknowledge the historical experiences of Black Americans and to support equal protection under the law, the label has been instrumental in advancing voting rights protections, anti-discrimination enforcement, and resource allocation aimed at reducing racial inequality. For decades, it has functioned as a tool of empowerment, helping to make racial disparities visible within American political and social institutions.

However, as demographic data increasingly influence electoral strategies, redistricting, and public policy debates, the political role of the “African American” category has evolved. What began as a mechanism for inclusion and civil rights accountability has also become central to identity-based political mobilization. In an era marked by heightened partisan polarization, demographic classifications are no longer viewed as neutral administrative tools. Instead, they often shape narratives about power, representation, and demographic change—sometimes fueling political division and racialized rhetoric.

This paper examines the evolving political implications of the “African American” demographic category, arguing that while it was designed to promote equity and representation, its continued use may also contribute to future political polarization. By analyzing the historical purpose, institutional function, and emerging political consequences of this classification, the study explores how demographic recognition can simultaneously empower marginalized communities and intensify identity-based political conflict in contemporary American politics.
